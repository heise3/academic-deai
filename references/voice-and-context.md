# Author voice and reader context

Read this reference when an author sample is supplied or when the reader's existing context changes what the edited text needs to explain.

## Use a sample without copying it

Use whatever representative sample the user already supplies; do not require a fixed number of papers. Infer stable choices in terminology, reporting verbs, argument progression, sentence rhythm, punctuation, and level of signposting. Preserve deliberate choices when they fit the requested audience and format. A sample guides style; it supplies no evidence for the new manuscript.

Do not imitate sentence lengths numerically, repeat the sample's mistakes, transplant its claims, or invent an opinion to create a personal voice. In academic prose, voice often comes from which evidence the author compares and how they explain a relation. First-person language is optional and discipline-dependent.

## Decide what the reader already knows

For a standalone manuscript, assume the reader has not seen the current conversation. Keep the background, definitions, study design, and necessary summaries that let the paper stand on its own. An abstract and a discussion may legitimately restate a key result for different purposes.

For a cover letter, review response, or explanation within a conversation, use the recipient's established context. Lead with the answer or change when that is what the reader needs. Retain identifiers, methodological details, and evidence needed to assess the response. Do not rewrite the whole study background merely because it was supplied with the request.

Every sentence should have a reader function: defining, reporting, comparing, qualifying, explaining, connecting, or summarizing. Novel information in every sentence is not a universal requirement.

## Keep genuine contrast and uncertainty

An invented contrast can make an ordinary claim sound grand. Remove that framing when its negative half adds no information. Preserve contrasts between study groups, competing mechanisms, measurement and inference, or a stated misconception. Preserve negative results, exclusion criteria, and explicit limits on causal interpretation.

English phrases such as `was associated with` are accurate statistical reporting language when the design warrants them. Keep meaningful `may`, `suggests`, `not`, and `only`. Rewriting a negative statement positively is appropriate only when the same proposition and limits survive.

## Worked examples

The following examples are invented language examples, not research results or benchmark outputs.

**Empty framing:** “This finding is not merely an observation; it marks a pivotal step toward understanding the system.”

Edit to the specific supported finding already present in the paragraph. If no such finding is supplied, do not manufacture one to fill the sentence.

**Necessary negation:** “The cross-sectional design does not establish whether poor sleep preceded itch.”

Keep the temporal and causal limit. A more direct equivalent is: “The cross-sectional design leaves the temporal order of poor sleep and itch unresolved.”

**Necessary parallelism:** “We recorded temperature, pressure, and flow every 10 seconds.”

Keep all three measurements and the interval. The number of items comes from the method.

**Voice sample:** An author consistently uses short result sentences followed by a longer explanation.

Use that rhythm when the evidence supports it. Do not force each paragraph into two sentences or insert an explanation the source lacks.
